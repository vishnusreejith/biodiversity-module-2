Introduction to Biodiversity

Understanding the variety of life on Earth.

Biodiversity (biological diversity) refers to the variety and variability of life on Earth. It is typically a measure of variation at the genetic, species, and ecosystem level.

It forms the web of life of which we are an integral part and upon which we so fully depend.

The Three Levels of Biodiversity

Genetic Diversity

The variation of genes within a species. It enables a population to adapt to its environment and respond to natural selection.

Example: Different varieties of mangoes or dogs.

Species Diversity

The variety of different species within a region. It is the most common level of diversity people think of.

Example: Tigers, deer, birds, and insects in a forest.

Ecosystem Diversity

The variety of habitats, biotic communities, and ecological processes in the biosphere.

Example: Deserts, wetlands, forests, and coral reefs.

Value & Levels of Biodiversity

The Value of Biodiversity

Consumptive Use

Directly consumed without passing through a market (e.g., firewood, hunted food, medicinal plants).

Productive Use

Commercially harvested goods sold in markets (e.g., timber, fish, agricultural crops, raw materials).

Social Value

Cultural, religious, and spiritual significance (e.g., sacred groves, holy rivers, community identity).

Ethical Value

The intrinsic right of all species to exist; humans have a moral responsibility to protect them.

Aesthetic Value

The beauty of nature, supporting ecotourism, recreation, and mental well-being.

Option Value

Potential undiscovered uses in the future (e.g., a cure for cancer from an unknown rainforest plant).

India: A Mega-Diversity Nation

India is one of the 17 mega-diverse countries globally.

  • Global Level: Shares many species with neighboring nations.
  • National Level: Contains ~7-8% of all recorded species despite having only 2.4% of land area.
  • Local Level: High point endemism in areas like Western Ghats.

Biodiversity Hotspots

Areas of extremely high biodiversity under extreme threat.

Criteria for a Hotspot (Norman Myers):

  1. Must contain at least 1,500 species of vascular plants (> 0.5% of the world's total) as endemics.
  2. Must have lost at least 70% of its original primary native vegetation.

Hotspots Encompassing India

1. The Western Ghats and Sri Lanka

Extremely rich in endemic plant and amphibian species. Faces severe pressure from agriculture and development.

2. The Eastern Himalayas

Known for abrupt topographical changes leading to high diversity. Contains many primitive flowering plants.

3. Indo-Burma (North-East)

Includes parts of North-East India. One of the most threatened hotspots due to rapid resource extraction.

4. Sundaland (Nicobar Islands)

A small portion extends into the Nicobar Islands. Rich in unique marine and terrestrial island biodiversity.

Threats & Species

Major Threats

Habitat Loss & Fragmentation

The #1 cause of extinction. Clearing land for agriculture or cities destroys habitats. Fragmentation breaks them into vulnerable patches.

Poaching of Wildlife

Illegal hunting for body parts (ivory, skin, horns), traditional medicine, or the exotic pet trade.

Man-Wildlife Conflict

Occurs when human populations expand into wildlife territory, leading to crop raiding by elephants or livestock predation by big cats.

Endangered Species

Species facing a very high risk of extinction in the wild.

  • Bengal Tiger
  • Asian Elephant
  • Great Indian Bustard
  • Snow Leopard
  • Gharial

Endemic Species

Species found *only* in a specific geographic location and nowhere else.

  • Lion-tailed Macaque (Western Ghats)
  • Nilgiri Tahr (Western Ghats)
  • Namdapha Flying Squirrel
  • Pygmy Hog (Assam)

Conservation & The Act

In-situ Conservation

"On-Site"

Conserving species within their natural habitats. It maintains recovering populations in the environment where they developed their distinctive properties.

Examples: National Parks, Wildlife Sanctuaries, Biosphere Reserves, Sacred Groves.

Ex-situ Conservation

"Off-Site"

Conserving species outside their natural habitats. Often used as a last resort or backup for highly endangered species.

Examples: Zoos, Botanical Gardens, Seed Banks, Gene Banks, Captive Breeding.

Biological Diversity Act, 2002

Enacted by India to meet obligations under the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD). It has a three-tiered structure: NBA (National), SBB (State), and BMC (Local).

Primary Objectives:

  1. Conservation of biological diversity.
  2. Sustainable use of its components.
  3. Fair and equitable sharing of benefits arising out of the use of biological resources and associated traditional knowledge.
